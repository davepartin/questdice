# QuestDice2: Nine Lanterns

*A fresh-eyes proposal. One of several independent pitches Dave asked for on 10 November 2026.*

Status: ideas only. No code, no rule changes, nothing tested at a table. Every number here is a first guess for a paper test, not a balance claim. I read DESIGN.md, TEAMPLAY.md, the magic card types, Monster Specials (T24) and the QuestDice2 concept notebook before writing. Where I disagree with something, I say so.

---

## The pitch in one breath

Your nine squares start the battle dark. Each round you **light one**. Lighting a square is drawing your sword, planting your feet, clearing your head. You will never light all nine, so every battle you become a slightly different hero. Meanwhile **the Dark gathers** on the monsters' side, so waiting has a price. Camp is where you decide what each square becomes when it is lit.

The game already says *"Roll your body. Break the dark."* This proposal takes that line literally. Heroes kindle light. Monsters gather dark. The battle is which one wins the race.

---

## Why rounds became "get some stuff, then lock in"

Dave's diagnosis is right, and I think there are three separate causes. Fixing only one will not be enough.

**The round has no question.** In Fleet Dice every round asks "do I grow or do I fight?" In QuestDice the board is the same in round six as in round one, so the best move is also the same: gather a bit of everything, lock in. Monster Specials help because they give the round a goal, but the hero's own board still does not change.

**The battle has no clock.** Fleet Dice works because the other captain is also building. If you grow too slowly, you lose. Against monsters that sit still, the right answer is always "build first, swing later," which is just a slower version of the same routine. Growth is only a decision if delay costs something.

**The team has no handoff.** Six heroes rolling side by side on their own phones are six solo games that share a health bar. Team triples and Heart to Heart are lovely when they happen, but they are luck you notice, not a plan you make. A team game needs moments where one player's choice changes what another player should do.

So the design below has three parts: a board that grows (the question), an enemy that grows too (the clock), and two small rules that make heroes need each other (the handoff).

## Part 1: The board that grows

### Start small, like Fleet Dice

Each battle begins with the five-die body cross live: Heart, Head, Feet and two Hands, all basic d4s around the d6 heart. The four corners are covered.

A hero is useful from the first throw. **A bare hand lets you choose** after the roll: strike with it (red) or guard with it (blue). That is simple, and it sets up a real trade later. A weapon adds its die, its bigger numbers and its icon faces, but the weapon die decides the colour. Bare hands are flexible. Weapons are strong and wilful.

### Kindle one square a round

Between rounds you may **kindle** one square. A lit square shows a small flame on its frame and changes for the rest of the battle.

- A **corner** uncovers. Upper corners are weapons ("draw your sword"). Lower corners are your prepared powers and talents.
- A **body square** steps up into the form you chose at camp. A hand grows from d4 to d6. A head gains an extra reroll die. Feet let you Step In for a friend (Part 3).

All nine squares can be lit. One kindle a round is the speed limit.

### The opening stance is free

Before the first throw, every hero kindles one square at no cost. This is the moment the Knight says "shield up" and the Ranger says "bow out." It gives every class its identity on throw one, it removes the feeling of a compulsory sword tax, and it starts the battle with a table conversation that takes five seconds.

### What it costs

After the opening, kindling costs the purple triangles. The price depends on **how many you have lit, not which square**: the second costs 2, then 3, then 4, then 5. Because every square costs the same, the only question is "which one do I want now," never "which one is cheaper."

With the current number language, four d4 body dice pay about 3 triangles a throw on average before the heart joins in (a 3 pays 2 and a 4 pays 1), and only about one throw in sixteen pays nothing before rerolls. If every triangle went to kindling, a hero would have five squares lit going into round five. Powers and paid rerolls want the same triangles, so a typical six-round battle ends with **four lit squares, five if you starve your powers**. That is the heart of the idea:

> **You never finish the grid.** Four or five lanterns out of nine. Which hero are you today?

Against armour you light the spear corner and your right hand. Against a swarm you light the splash power and your head. The same Knight plays two different battles.

### Lines: tic-tac-toe on your own body

QuestDice already says "every row attacks, every column guards." Let the lit squares use the same rule. When three lit squares make a row or a column, that is a **Flare**:

- A lit **row** flares for attack. A lit **column** flares for guard. One big moment (the size of a triple) on the round it completes.
- Each Flare **pushes the Dark back one step** (Part 2).

This gives build order a shape without forcing it. Five lanterns can be a row plus a column that cross. A player who wants the Flare on round three plans for it from the opening stance. And it is readable from across the table: "she is one square from a line."

### The number language finally means three different things

This is the part I am most excited about, because it costs nothing new. The existing faces already point at three different horizons. They just never competed before.

| You rolled | It pays | When it helps |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Pierce | **This round** |
| 3 or 4 | Purple triangles | **This battle** (kindle a square, or use a power now) |
| 1 | Gold | **This campaign** (camp) |
| High hand numbers | Raw strength | **This round** |

Dave wrote, "I am trying to roll for gold, I am trying to roll for skills." That is exactly this table. Once triangles buy growth, a reroll stops being "fix my worst die" and becomes "what do I need most: the hit, the lantern, or the purse?"

### A name for the triangles

My suggestion is **Spark**. The game already has a Spark symbol that produces Magic, so the word is half taught. It fits the light theme ("roll for Sparks, spend Sparks to kindle"), and it works for a knight as well as a wizard. Focus, from the concept notebook, is also good and I would be content with it. I would avoid Skill, because spending skill sounds like forgetting something.

## Part 2: The Dark gathers

The monsters need to warm up too, or the growing board becomes a shopping list with one correct order.

Put a short **Dark track** beside the monsters, five or six steps, drawn as a moon rising or candles going out. It moves one step every round. At marked steps, something visible happens: the monsters' power die grows a size, then a size again, and at the last step, Midnight, they strike twice.

Three things follow from one small track.

**Growing now costs something.** Every round spent building is a round closer to Midnight. The team can feel it.

**Nobody farms the last goblin.** Stalling is punished by the track, so no hidden anti-farming rules are needed.

**The peak stays short and sweet.** The battle is pushed to end around rounds five to seven, which leaves two or three rounds to enjoy the hero you built without sliding back into sameness.

Because a Flare pushes the Dark back one step, the two halves of the game talk to each other. Light literally holds off the dark. A party that plans its lines buys itself another round.

Monster Specials sit on top of this unchanged. The Special is this round's question. The Dark is the battle's question.

Existing monster tricks get a new, better target too. **Bind** can *dim a lit square* for a round instead of locking a die. Losing your sword arm for one round is a story. A boss at half health could snuff one lantern from every hero.

## Part 3: A team that needs each other

Two rules. Both are one tap or less, and both happen inside the simultaneous round the game already has.

### Called targets and Step In

Right now the lowest Feet draws the attack, and you find out after you have rolled. Flip the timing: when the monsters show their intention, they also **show who they are coming for**. "The Ogre eyes the Wizard."

Now the table has a one-sentence conversation before the dice are thrown: "I've got her." A hero may **Step In** by spending their Feet die that round (it pays nothing else), and take the hit instead. Then everyone rolls, and the whole table watches the Knight's block dice, because the Wizard's turn depends on them.

This is the D&D moment Dave is chasing: a friend jumping in front of a blow. It needs no map. It makes Feet matter every round. And it creates the natural team rhythm of a growing board: **someone holds the line while someone else builds.**

### Openings: one mark, set up by one hero, cashed by another

Add a single mark a monster can carry: an **Opening**. Some weapon icons and powers *make* an Opening (a shield bash, a snare, a discord). Others *cash* it for a bonus (an aimed shot pierces double, a cleave splashes in full). One mark, one slot per monster, gone when cashed.

That is the whole combo system. "I trip him, you stab him," with one icon to learn. Any class can be the setter or the finisher depending on which squares they lit this battle, so roles come from choices rather than being printed on the class.

### Company in Stride

When **every** hero has three lanterns lit, the company is in stride, and team triples and Heart to Heart pay double for the rest of the battle. The effect is that the strong player has a reason to help the slow one. Share Magic, which already exists, suddenly has a purpose: pass two Sparks to the Bard so the whole table tips over.

### Second Wind

The first time a hero drops below half health, they kindle one square free. It is grace for the one taking the beating, and a small reward for stepping in.

## Part 4: Camp and the character sheet

Here is the D&D-light part, and it stays on one screen.

**Your character sheet is the 3×3 grid.** Each square has a slot for what it becomes when lit. Building a character means filling nine slots. That is the whole sheet.

| Square | What you slot there | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Upper corners | Weapons | Sword, shield, bow (a two-hander fills both and lights with one kindle at a higher price) |
| Lower corners | Powers and talents | The existing magic cards: a splash, a risky big swing, a steady pierce |
| Hands | Strength forms | "Grows to d6." Later "grows to d8," or "d6 and every 2 pierces one more" |
| Head | Tactics | An extra reroll die. See the monsters' power die early. |
| Heart | Courage | Turn the heart once a round for free. A stronger 5 and 6. |
| Feet | Footwork | Step In without giving up the Feet payout. Step In and make an Opening. |

This gives camp three clean kinds of purchase, and all three are what Dave described as "upgrading the upgrade options."

1. **Gear (gold).** A new weapon for a corner. You own it forever. It still starts the battle covered.
2. **Training (level-ups).** Each level, choose one new form from three offered, for any square. This replaces the perk list. A perk is a number you forget you have. A form is a square you get to light.
3. **Readiness (gold, expensive, rare).** More starting health. Or the big one: **Second Nature**, where one chosen square starts every battle already lit. Earn the first around level 5 and a second around level 10. That is the "one small thing you start with that is better than the last," and it feels like a veteran who walks in with sword already drawn.

The 39 magic cards and their five templates carry over as they are. The only change is where they live: a power sits in a lower corner (or a body square, for the every-round ones) and comes online when its square is lit. Finisher cards change their gate from "round 3 or later" to "four lanterns lit," which ties the big moment to the hero's own climb.

The loadout rule stays small. Nine slots, one form each. Learning more forms widens your options at camp. It never adds buttons to the battle screen.

## What one battle might feel like

Two heroes, a Knight and a Ranger, against an armoured Ogre and two goblins. Illustrative, not tuned.

**Opening.** The Ogre eyes the Ranger. The Knight opens with shield. The Ranger opens with bow. "I've got you."

**Round 1.** The Knight Steps In and guards with a bare right hand as well. The Ranger rolls two 3s and keeps them: Sparks over damage this round.

**Round 2.** The Ranger kindles her splash power because the goblins are still up. The Knight kindles his left hand to d6. The Dark reaches its first mark, and the Ogre's die grows.

**Round 3.** The Knight's shield bash makes an Opening on the Ogre. The Ranger cashes it with a doubled pierce. The Knight lights his lower-left corner and completes his left column: Flare. A wall of block, and the Dark slides back a step.

**Round 4.** Both heroes have three lanterns. Company in Stride. They turn their hearts to match and aim at the Ogre together.

**Round 5.** The Ranger has four lit. Rain of Arrows is live. The story the table tells afterwards is "I was going to draw my sword, but the goblins were still alive, so I lit the splash instead."

## The map, on a napkin

Dave shelved the map, and I agree with the reason: grids slow a team down. I would still keep one small idea in a drawer, because it gives most of what Epic Duels' board gives for almost no cost.

**Two rows instead of a map.** Each hero is in the Front or the Back. Stepping between them costs your Feet die. Melee weapons need the Front. Bows and staves work from the Back. Monsters must go through the Front before they can call a target in the Back, unless they have reach. That is range, melee and protection with one switch per hero and no pathfinding to argue about.

I would not build it first. Called targets and Step In may already give the feeling of position. If the paper test says players still want to "be somewhere," this is the cheapest next step.

## Smaller sparks

- **Duels come free.** A board that grows round by round is Fleet Dice's shape, so two heroes can fight each other with these same rules. A camp sparring ring between friends would reuse everything.
- **Battle memory.** After a win, show the grid as it ended: "Tonight you were Shield, Left Hand, Bash, Head." People will screenshot it.
- **Mastery.** Light the same form in ten battles and it gains a small name and a gold frame. Development that comes from playing, not buying.
- **Elite monsters carry their own lanterns.** A boss with a visible grid that lights a square at each Dark mark is the Fleet Dice rival come back, and it teaches players to read the enemy the way they read themselves.
- **Road events can touch the grid.** "You slept badly: your Head cannot be kindled in the next battle." "A blessing at the shrine: your opening stance lights two squares."

## What I would leave out of the first version

Being honest about scope matters more than being generous with ideas.

- **Perks as a separate list.** Fold the good ones into forms.
- **Buying bigger dice as flat permanent upgrades.** Die size becomes "what this hand grows into when lit."
- **Straights.** They are fun, but they reward the whole board at once, and the board is now partial for most of a battle. Bring them back later if they are missed.
- **Secret objectives.** Rest them until the basic climb is fun. A gold-seeking objective during the build-up would pull a hero away from the team at the worst moment.
- **Charms** stay resting, as already decided.

## Where this differs from the concept notebook

The notebook and I agree on the foundation: body cross, covered corners, growth inside the battle, camp improving the options. I differ on five points.

1. **An enemy clock is not optional.** The notebook treats escalation as something to add "only if testing shows the need." I think growth without a clock cannot produce a real decision, and I would put the Dark track in the very first paper test.
2. **Price by count, not by square**, with a free opening stance. This removes the sword tax and resource drought in round one without a catch-up subsystem.
3. **All nine squares can be lit, and you never finish.** The notebook grows toward a full board. I think the partial board is the feature.
4. **The team rules come first, not later.** Called targets, Step In and Openings are what make this a team game instead of parallel solitaire.
5. **The character sheet is the grid.** One picture for build, battle and memory.

---

## Risks, said plainly

- **Too many new ideas at once.** Kindling, the Dark, called targets, Openings and lines are five. Test kindling and the Dark together first, since each needs the other. Add called targets second. Openings and lines last.
- **Round one may feel thin.** Five d4s and one lit square is a small hero. If the first two rounds drag, start with two free kindles.
- **Step In could make one player the permanent bodyguard.** If so, limit it (not two rounds running) or let the Dark occasionally call two targets.
- **Flares pushing the Dark back might let a careful party stall forever.** Cap it at one push per hero per battle.
- **Bare hands that choose their colour may be too good.** If nobody draws a weapon, weapons need more punch, or bare hands need a fixed side.
- **I have not played this.** The arithmetic above is from the tables in DESIGN.md. The fun is a guess until dice hit a table.

## A twenty-minute paper test

Two players, real dice, nine sticky notes each.

1. Each player takes a d6 heart and four d4s for head, feet and hands. Cover the corners. Use the current number language.
2. Give each player four forms on sticky notes: one weapon corner, one power corner, "hand grows to d6," and "head: one more reroll die."
3. One monster with 40 health that hits for d6, with a Monster Special if you like. Put a coin on a five-step track and move it every round. At steps 3 and 5 its die grows a size.
4. Free opening kindle. After that, 2, 3, 4, 5 Sparks.
5. The monster names its target before the throw. The other player may Step In with their Feet die.
6. Play it twice. Then play once more with **no** Dark track and see whether the choices go flat.

Afterwards, ask three things. Did you change your plan mid-battle? Did you say anything to your friend before rolling? Which round was the best one?

If the answers are yes, yes, and "round four," this is worth building.
